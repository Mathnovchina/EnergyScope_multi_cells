# Effects of Physical Forest Disturbances on Wood Availability and Prices in Europe, Boreal Forests, and Finland

## Executive Summary

Physical disturbances—bark beetle outbreaks, windstorms, and wildfires—have profoundly reshaped European timber markets over the past two decades, with cascading effects on wood availability, prices, and forest value chains. This review synthesizes evidence from 121 peer-reviewed studies to examine how these disturbances affect roundwood markets across Europe, with particular attention to boreal forests and Finland. The evidence reveals that disturbances trigger acute supply shocks that depress timber prices by 20–70%, disrupt long-term forest management, and challenge the economic viability of forestry operations. In Central Europe, bark beetle outbreaks have generated nearly 100 million m³ of unplanned harvests over the past decade, driving spruce prices to historical lows. Major windstorms such as Gudrun (Sweden, 75 million m³), Lothar and Martin (France, 176 million m³), and Vaia (Italy, 8.5 million m³) have caused immediate market saturation and price collapses of 35–69%. While Finland has experienced comparatively limited direct impacts to date, salvage logging costs are 10–64% higher than normal operations, and the country faces growing vulnerability as climate change intensifies disturbance regimes. The findings underscore the need for adaptive forest management, enhanced processing capacity, strategic timber storage, and coordinated policy responses to maintain market stability and forest sector resilience in an era of accelerating environmental change.

## 1. Introduction

European forests face unprecedented pressures from climate-driven physical disturbances that fundamentally alter timber supply dynamics and market equilibrium. Bark beetle outbreaks, windstorms, and wildfires have intensified markedly since 1950, with disturbances now accounting for approximately 16% of mean annual harvest across Europe and generating an average of 43.8 million m³ of disturbed timber annually [21]. These disturbances create sudden, involuntary supply shocks that disrupt long-term forest management planning, overwhelm processing capacity, and trigger sharp price adjustments in regional and international roundwood markets [1], [21].

The economic consequences extend beyond immediate price volatility. Forest owners face reduced profitability, diminished management motivation, and in extreme cases, abandonment of active forest stewardship [6], [11]. Processing industries confront quality degradation, logistical challenges, and inventory management difficulties [11], [19]. At the policy level, governments have intervened with hundreds of millions of euros in emergency support to stabilize affected regions [8].

This review addresses three central questions: (1) How do different types of physical disturbances—bark beetles, windstorms, and wildfires—affect wood availability and timber prices in European markets? (2) What are the specific experiences and vulnerabilities of boreal forests and Finland within this broader European context? (3) What mechanisms explain market responses, and what adaptive strategies can enhance forest sector resilience?

Drawing on a comprehensive evidence base of 121 studies spanning multiple disturbance events, geographic regions, and analytical approaches, this review provides a deep comparative analysis of disturbance impacts on timber economics. Particular attention is given to Central European bark beetle crises, major windstorm events across the continent, emerging wildfire risks, and the distinctive characteristics of Nordic and Finnish forest systems.

## 2. Background and Theoretical Foundations

Forest disturbances operate as exogenous supply shocks in timber markets, fundamentally altering the supply-demand equilibrium through several interconnected mechanisms. When disturbances damage standing timber, forest owners face strong incentives to harvest affected trees rapidly to recover economic value before wood quality deteriorates further and to prevent secondary damage such as bark beetle spread [3], [27]. This creates a sudden rightward shift in the timber supply curve, increasing the quantity of wood entering markets while simultaneously depressing prices [1], [12].

The magnitude and duration of price effects depend on multiple factors: the scale and spatial concentration of disturbance, the capacity of processing industries to absorb sudden supply increases, the availability of storage infrastructure, the quality and assortment mix of salvaged timber, and the integration of affected regions into broader national and international markets [12], [19], [23]. Large-scale disturbances that exceed regional processing capacity generate more severe and prolonged price depressions than smaller, localized events [2], [6].

Salvage logging introduces additional economic complexities. Damaged timber must be harvested under suboptimal conditions—often on steep terrain, with compromised tree stability, and under time pressure—resulting in substantially higher harvesting costs and lower productivity compared to planned operations [3], [19]. Quality degradation from insect damage, fire scorching, or fungal colonization further reduces the value of salvaged wood and may shift assortment distributions toward lower-value products [11], [22].

Historical evidence from European forests demonstrates that natural disturbances have long influenced forest dynamics, but their frequency, intensity, and economic impacts have increased significantly since the mid-20th century [20], [21]. Climate change acts as a threat multiplier, increasing the likelihood of extreme weather events, extending bark beetle reproductive seasons, and creating conditions conducive to larger, more severe wildfires [16], [36], [38]. This intensification occurs against a backdrop of forests managed primarily for timber production, often with simplified species compositions that may increase vulnerability to specific disturbance agents [8], [37].

The concept of forest value chain resilience has emerged as a framework for understanding how forest sectors respond to and recover from disturbances [4]. Resilience depends on the adaptive capacity of forest owners, the flexibility of processing industries, the diversity of market channels, and the effectiveness of policy interventions [4], [6]. Regions with proactive management, diversified processing capacity, and coordinated response mechanisms demonstrate greater stability in the face of disturbances than those relying on reactive, market-driven approaches [4].

## 3. Disturbance Regimes and Their Intensification

Natural disturbances in European forests have increased substantially in both frequency and severity over recent decades. Analysis of disturbance patterns from 1950 to 2019 reveals a significant upward trend, with wind, fire, and bark beetles emerging as the dominant agents [21]. Disturbances now generate an average of 43.8 million m³ of damaged timber annually, with unreported disturbances potentially adding another 8.6 to 18.3 million m³ per year [21]. This represents approximately 16% of mean annual harvest across Europe over the last two decades, underscoring the magnitude of disturbance-driven timber supply [21].

Wind disturbances constitute the most volumetrically significant disturbance type in many European regions. Major storm events such as Lothar and Martin in December 1999 damaged approximately 180 million m³ of timber across multiple countries, with France alone experiencing 176 million m³ of damage valued at 6 to 7 billion euros [1], [19]. Storm Gudrun in 2005 damaged 75 million m³ in Sweden [9]. More recently, storm Vaia in 2018 damaged 8.5 million m³ in northeastern Italy [2]. These catastrophic events create immediate market crises, overwhelming regional processing capacity and triggering sharp price declines [1], [2], [12].

Bark beetle outbreaks, particularly of the European spruce bark beetle (*Ips typographus*), have intensified dramatically in Central Europe since 2015. The Czech Republic experienced a devastating outbreak that generated nearly 100 million m³ of unplanned harvests over a decade, with state interventions totaling approximately 260 million EUR in 2018–2019 alone [5], [8]. Austria recorded two unprecedented outbreaks since 2015, with the first peaking at 3.3 million m³ (2.6% of total growing stock) in 2018 and the second reaching 1.7 million m³ in 2022 [17]. These outbreaks are strongly linked to climate drivers, including drought stress, warm temperatures that accelerate beetle development, and predisposing wind disturbances that create breeding material [8], [13], [16].

Wildfire impacts, while historically concentrated in Mediterranean regions, are expanding northward as climate change creates more favorable fire weather conditions [39]. In Mediterranean France, bioeconomic projections indicate moderate losses of merchantable timber with limited industrial implications but notable price increases, especially for softwoods [7]. Boreal forests face increasing wildfire risk, with climate-driven changes in fire weather potentially altering fire regimes significantly [32].

The interaction between disturbance types amplifies overall impacts. Windstorms create large volumes of downed wood that serve as breeding substrate for bark beetles, triggering secondary outbreaks that can exceed the initial storm damage [30], [37]. Drought stress weakens tree defenses against bark beetles while simultaneously increasing wildfire risk [8], [36]. This cascading nature of disturbances complicates management responses and extends the duration of market disruptions [8], [19].

## 4. Economic Impacts on Timber Markets: A Comparative Analysis

### 4.1 Bark Beetle Outbreaks

Bark beetle outbreaks generate sustained, multi-year supply shocks that fundamentally destabilize timber markets. The Czech Republic provides the most extensively documented case, where bark beetle-driven calamity logging produced nearly 100 million m³ of unplanned harvests over the past decade [5]. This massive supply increase drove spruce wood prices to historical lows, with average prices for spruce/fir (category SM/JE II) projected to reach EUR 79.39 per m³, and total forest sector losses estimated at EUR 1.12 billion [5]. Market disruptions extended beyond prices to affect forestry companies, workers, and management practices, including wage stagnation [5].

In Northern Austria, bark beetle outbreaks associated with water shortage caused regional market prices for beetle-affected timber to decline to 30% of previous levels [11]. Sawmills accumulated excessive inventories of unprocessed logs, and small forest owners lost motivation for active forest management [11]. Large forest enterprises absorbed higher management costs to maintain operations, but the economic strain was substantial [11]. The outbreak also reduced Austria's forest carbon sink strength from 12% to 6% of the national greenhouse gas emission budget, illustrating broader environmental consequences [11].

The sustained nature of bark beetle outbreaks distinguishes them from acute windstorm events. While storms create immediate but time-limited supply pulses, bark beetle populations can build over multiple years, generating continuous pressure on markets [8], [37]. This prolonged supply increase prevents normal price recovery and creates chronic profitability challenges for forest owners [6], [11]. State interventions, including subsidies for salvage operations and market support measures, have been necessary to prevent complete market collapse in severely affected regions [8].

Bark beetle damage also affects timber quality and assortment distributions. Blue-stain fungi associated with bark beetles reduce the aesthetic and structural quality of wood, shifting salvaged timber toward lower-value products [22]. This quality degradation compounds the price effects of oversupply, further reducing revenues for forest owners [11], [22].

### 4.2 Windstorm Disturbances

Windstorms create acute, high-volume supply shocks that trigger immediate and severe price depressions. The Vaia storm in northeastern Italy provides a well-documented recent example. The storm overloaded local markets with massive roundwood volumes, causing spruce stumpage prices to decline by 69% in Trento and 57% in Veneto, while roadside prices fell by 37% in Trento and Bolzano and 39% in Friuli Venezia Giulia [2]. Total roundwood sales volume in Trento increased by 734%, illustrating the magnitude of the supply shock [2]. Standing sales suffered higher losses than roadside sales due to the costs and duration of salvage operations [2].

Storm Lothar in Germany caused a corrected price fall of 25.8 €/m³ for spruce in Baden-Württemberg between the year before the storm and the storm year, with the strongest price fall reaching 29.34 €/m³ [12]. In Austria following a 1990 storm, sawlog prices dropped 20% within two months [1]. The December 1999 storms Lothar and Martin across multiple European countries created estimated total damage of 180 million m³ and economic losses of approximately ten billion euros [19].

The temporal dynamics of windstorm price effects follow a characteristic pattern. Prices typically drop sharply immediately following the storm as salvage operations flood markets [1], [12]. In France after the 1999 storms, strategic commercialization networks and delayed supply limited average timber price decreases to 35–38% instead of the predicted 50% [19]. This demonstrates that coordinated market management can moderate price impacts, though substantial losses remain unavoidable [19].

Salvage logging costs increase dramatically after windstorms. Cutting productivity of windfalls is 19–33% lower than that of undamaged stems, while cutting costs are 35–64% higher [3]. Logging costs overall are 10–30% higher for windthrown timber compared to undamaged standing stems [3]. These increased costs, combined with depressed prices, severely compress profit margins for forest owners and contractors [3], [19].

The spatial extent of windstorms influences market impacts. Large storms affecting multiple regions or countries can saturate national and international markets, limiting the ability of unaffected regions to absorb excess supply [9], [19]. Conversely, more localized storms may allow for timber export to neighboring regions, moderating local price effects [2].

### 4.3 Wildfire Impacts

Wildfire impacts on timber markets differ substantially from bark beetle and windstorm effects, with outcomes varying by fire severity, forest type, and regional market structure. In Mediterranean France, bioeconomic projections indicate that climate-induced wildfires will cause moderate losses of merchantable timber with limited implications for industries, but will generate price increases, especially for softwoods [7]. This contrasts sharply with the price depressions observed following bark beetle outbreaks and windstorms, reflecting differences in the nature of supply shocks [7].

The Biscuit Fire in southwest Oregon, which burned nearly 500,000 acres in 2002, provides insights into wildfire price dynamics. The fire did not cause immediate price effects, but positive price effects were detected during the salvage logging period in some markets, followed by a negative long-run effect persisting after salvage completion [24]. This temporal pattern suggests that salvage operations can temporarily tighten markets by diverting harvesting capacity, followed by longer-term supply increases as salvaged timber enters markets [24].

Salvage logging costs for burned forests vary with site characteristics. Analysis of wildfire salvage on Turkey's Mediterranean coast found that slope position, aspect, stand age, crown closure, and burn severity significantly impact pricing, with these factors explaining 75.2% of price variation [25]. This highlights the heterogeneity of wildfire impacts and the importance of site-specific economic assessments [25].

In boreal forests, wildfire risk is projected to increase substantially under climate change scenarios [32]. While current wildfire impacts on European boreal timber markets remain limited compared to bark beetle and windstorm effects, the potential for future disruptions is significant [32], [36]. Boreal wildfires can affect vast areas and may generate supply shocks of different character than those observed in temperate and Mediterranean regions [32].

## 5. Regional Perspectives: Europe, Boreal Forests, and Finland

### 5.1 Central European Experiences

Central Europe has experienced the most severe and sustained disturbance impacts on timber markets over the past two decades, driven primarily by bark beetle outbreaks in Norway spruce-dominated forests. The Czech Republic represents the epicenter of this crisis, with bark beetle-driven calamity logging generating nearly 100 million m³ of unplanned harvests over the past decade [5]. This unprecedented outbreak was triggered by a combination of drought stress, warm temperatures, and predisposing wind disturbances that created ideal conditions for bark beetle population explosions [8], [13].

The economic consequences for Czech forestry have been catastrophic. Spruce wood prices declined to historical lows, with average prices for spruce/fir (category SM/JE II) projected to reach EUR 79.39 per m³, and total forest sector losses estimated at EUR 1.12 billion [5]. State interventions amounted to approximately 260 million EUR in 2018–2019 alone, reflecting the scale of the crisis [8]. Market disruptions extended beyond prices to affect employment, wages, and the viability of forest management operations [5].

Austria has similarly experienced unprecedented bark beetle outbreaks since 2015, with two distinct outbreak waves [17]. The first outbreak peaked in 2018 at 3.3 million m³ (2.6% of total growing stock), while the second reached 1.7 million m³ in 2022 [17]. In Northern Austria, regional market prices for beetle-affected timber declined to 30% of previous levels, sawmills accumulated excessive inventories, and small forest owners lost motivation for active management [11].

Germany has faced combined pressures from both windstorms and subsequent bark beetle outbreaks. Storm Lothar caused severe immediate price impacts, with spruce prices falling by 25.8 €/m³ in Baden-Württemberg [12]. The storm-damaged timber then served as breeding substrate for bark beetles, extending the duration of market disruptions [12].

These Central European experiences reveal several common patterns: (1) the cascading nature of disturbances, with wind damage predisposing forests to bark beetle outbreaks; (2) the sustained, multi-year duration of bark beetle-driven supply shocks; (3) the inadequacy of regional processing capacity to absorb sudden supply increases; (4) the severe compression of forest owner profitability leading to reduced management motivation; and (5) the necessity of substantial state interventions to prevent complete market collapse [5], [8], [11], [12].

### 5.2 Nordic and Boreal Forest Contexts

Nordic and boreal forest regions present a contrasting picture to Central Europe, with generally lower disturbance impacts to date but growing vulnerabilities. Sweden experienced major windstorm damage from storm Gudrun in 2005, which damaged 75 million m³ of timber [9]. This created supply peaks followed by local shortages, illustrating the temporal dynamics of large-scale windstorm impacts [9]. However, Sweden has not experienced bark beetle outbreaks of the magnitude seen in Central Europe, partly due to different climatic conditions and forest management practices [10].

The Swedish forest-based sector faces broader structural challenges beyond immediate disturbance impacts. Volume-based manufacturing is expected to stagnate or decline due to high costs and weak EU demand, with bulk production projected to shift to the Global South [15]. Long-term viability requires a strategic shift to high-value segments and advanced technologies [15]. This structural transition occurs against a backdrop of climate change impacts and forest disturbances that may intensify in coming decades [15].

Boreal forests across Fennoscandia face increasing climate-driven risks. Climate change projections indicate multiple risks including increased storm damage, extended bark beetle reproductive seasons, and elevated wildfire probability [36], [38]. Warmer summers may enable two bark beetle generations per year instead of one, doubling the potential for population growth and forest damage [40]. However, colder winter temperatures in boreal regions currently limit bark beetle survival compared to Central Europe [33], [40].

The ecological characteristics of boreal forests differ from Central European temperate forests in ways that influence disturbance vulnerability and market impacts. Boreal forests typically have lower productivity, longer rotation periods, and different species compositions [31], [34]. These factors affect both the economic value of disturbance-damaged timber and the capacity of forests to recover from disturbances [34].

### 5.3 Finland-Specific Evidence

Finland has experienced relatively limited direct disturbance impacts on timber markets compared to Central Europe, but faces growing vulnerabilities and has valuable experience with salvage logging operations. A comparative study of European spruce bark beetle damage prevention found significant differences between Central Europe and Finland, with bark beetle damages playing a less prominent role in Finnish timber markets to date [10]. However, salvage logging remains an important method for dealing with beetle-damaged wood when outbreaks do occur [10].

Detailed analysis of salvage logging productivity and costs in windthrown Norway spruce-dominated forests in Finland reveals the economic challenges of post-disturbance operations [3]. Cutting productivity of windfalls was 19–33% lower than that of undamaged stems, while cutting costs were 35–64% higher [3]. Logging costs overall were 10–30% higher for windthrown timber compared to undamaged standing stems [3]. These findings quantify the economic burden of salvage operations and highlight the importance of rapid response to minimize bark beetle colonization of damaged timber [3].

Finland's forest value chain demonstrated prolonged instability in response to climate disturbances in a comparative study of five European countries [4]. Unlike the Czech Republic, Croatia, and Spain, which maintained relative stability through proactive management and processing capacities, Finland and Germany experienced prolonged instability due to market-driven logging and limited adaptive measures [4]. This suggests that despite lower absolute disturbance levels, Finland's forest sector may be vulnerable to disruptions when disturbances do occur [4].

Climate change projections for Finland indicate multiple risks to boreal forests and forestry, including increased storm damage, bark beetle outbreaks, and wildfire probability [36], [38]. Warmer temperatures may extend the bark beetle reproductive season and increase population growth rates, while changes in precipitation patterns could increase both drought stress and storm intensity [36], [38]. These projected changes suggest that Finland's historically low disturbance impacts may not persist under future climate scenarios [36], [38].

Finland's forest sector operates within a broader context of intensive timber production and biodiversity concerns. Analysis of sustainable harvest levels indicates that maintaining favorable reference values for old-growth forest cover, deadwood volumes, and deciduous tree proportions would require reducing maximum economic sustainable harvest levels from the current 96% to 60% [34]. This tension between production objectives and ecological sustainability may be exacerbated by climate-driven disturbances that further constrain management options [34].

The Finnish roundwood market has historically been influenced by domestic supply-demand dynamics, with econometric models identifying key relationships between prices, harvests, and market conditions [28], [29]. However, these models were developed during periods of relatively stable disturbance regimes and may require updating to account for increasing climate-driven disruptions [28], [29]. Integration into European and global markets means that disturbances in other regions can also affect Finnish timber prices through international trade linkages [9], [41].

## 6. Salvage Logging Economics and Operational Challenges

Salvage logging operations face fundamental economic and operational challenges that distinguish them from planned harvesting activities. The primary objective of salvage logging is to recover economic value from damaged timber before quality deteriorates further and to prevent secondary damage such as bark beetle spread [3], [27]. However, achieving these objectives requires operating under suboptimal conditions that substantially increase costs and reduce productivity [3], [19].

Productivity losses in salvage operations are substantial and well-documented. In Finnish windthrown Norway spruce forests, cutting productivity of windfalls was 19–33% lower than that of undamaged stems [3]. This productivity reduction reflects multiple factors: unstable tree positions that complicate felling and processing, root systems that may still partially anchor trees, and the need for careful operator positioning to avoid safety hazards [3]. The variability in productivity (19–33% reduction) depends on specific site conditions, damage patterns, and the size of windthrown stems [3].

Cost increases compound productivity losses. Cutting costs for windthrown stems (0.3–1.5 m³) were 35–64% higher than for undamaged standing stems in Finland [3]. Overall logging costs were 10–30% higher for windfalls compared to undamaged timber [3]. These cost increases reflect not only reduced productivity but also higher equipment wear, increased fuel consumption, and elevated safety risks [3]. After major windstorms in Europe, logging costs have been observed to increase greatly, further compressing profit margins for forest owners and contractors [19].

The economics of salvage logging vary substantially with disturbance type and severity. For wildfire salvage on Turkey's Mediterranean coast, slope position, aspect, stand age, crown closure, and burn severity significantly impacted pricing, explaining 75.2% of price variation [25]. This heterogeneity means that salvage operations must be evaluated on a site-specific basis, with some areas potentially uneconomical to harvest [25], [26].

Timber quality degradation represents an additional economic challenge. Bark beetle-infested timber suffers from blue-stain fungi that reduce aesthetic and structural quality, shifting salvaged wood toward lower-value products [22]. Fire-damaged timber may have reduced strength properties and increased checking, limiting its suitability for high-value applications [25]. Wind-damaged timber quality depends on the time elapsed between damage and salvage, with rapid deterioration if bark beetles colonize downed trees [3], [27].

The temporal urgency of salvage operations creates logistical and market challenges. Damaged timber must be harvested rapidly to prevent quality loss and bark beetle colonization, but this urgency coincides with sudden supply increases that depress prices [1], [3]. Forest owners face a difficult trade-off: delay salvage to wait for better prices and risk further quality deterioration, or harvest immediately and accept depressed prices [1], [12]. This dilemma is particularly acute for small forest owners with limited financial reserves [11].

Storage strategies can help moderate market impacts by spreading salvage timber sales over time. Analysis of timber stock residence times in storage after severe storm events in Germany found that storage can delay market entry and reduce price volatility [12]. However, storage requires infrastructure investment, incurs holding costs, and is only feasible for certain timber assortments and qualities [12]. Water storage (wet storage) can preserve log quality for extended periods but requires suitable facilities and may face environmental constraints [12].

## 7. Value Chain Resilience and Market Dynamics

Forest value chain resilience—the capacity of forest sectors to absorb, adapt to, and recover from disturbances—varies substantially across European regions and depends on multiple interacting factors [4]. Comparative analysis of five European countries (Czech Republic, Croatia, Germany, Finland, and Spain) revealed that climate disturbances cause timber supply fluctuations in quantity and quality, with high-volume salvage logging frequently breaching resilience thresholds [4].

The Czech Republic, Croatia, and Spain maintained relative stability through proactive management and processing capacities [4]. These countries demonstrated adaptive capacity by rapidly mobilizing salvage operations, coordinating market responses, and leveraging processing flexibility to absorb sudden supply increases [4]. In contrast, Germany and Finland experienced prolonged instability due to market-driven logging and limited adaptive measures [4]. This divergence highlights the importance of institutional capacity, processing infrastructure, and coordinated governance in maintaining value chain resilience [4].

Processing capacity emerges as a critical determinant of market stability following disturbances. When salvage timber volumes exceed regional processing capacity, markets become saturated and prices collapse [2], [11]. In northeastern Italy after storm Vaia, local markets were overloaded with massive roundwood volumes, causing spruce stumpage prices to decline by 69% in Trento and 57% in Veneto [2]. The inability of local sawmills to process the sudden supply increase forced timber sales at severely depressed prices [2].

Market integration and trade linkages influence how disturbance impacts propagate across regions. Large disturbances affecting multiple countries can saturate national and international markets, limiting the ability of unaffected regions to absorb excess supply [9], [19]. Conversely, well-integrated markets may allow for timber export to less-affected regions, moderating local price effects [2]. However, trade also means that disturbances in one region can affect prices in distant markets through supply chain adjustments [23].

The concept of market tipping points has emerged to describe threshold effects where disturbances trigger structural market changes rather than temporary disruptions [6]. Simulation modeling of the Austrian wood value chain found that increased forest disturbances lead to reduced roundwood prices and significant challenges for forest owners, with lack of profitability causing reduced management motivation and potential forest management abandonment [6]. Tipping points result in structural changes such as reduced roundwood and product production, alongside reduced profitability for forest owners and industry [6].

Intensifying droughts, storms, and pest outbreaks alter forest productivity and stability, producing significant fluctuations in wood supply and price dynamics [14]. These fluctuations generate economic vulnerabilities extending across value chains and undermine long-term investment certainty [14]. The unpredictability of disturbance timing and magnitude complicates business planning for both forest owners and processing industries [14].

International market dynamics add further complexity. Average annual wind damage in the United States contributes downward pressure of 1% to 4% on roundwood prices, with effects persisting for approximately 25 years [23]. Unaffected regions change harvesting patterns to compensate for fiber availability changes, distributing impacts internationally via interconnected supply chains [23]. This demonstrates that disturbance impacts are not confined to directly affected regions but propagate through global timber markets [23].

## 8. Discussion: Mechanisms, Vulnerabilities, and Adaptive Capacity

The evidence synthesized in this review reveals several fundamental mechanisms through which physical disturbances affect wood availability and timber prices. First, disturbances create involuntary supply shocks that shift the supply curve rightward, increasing quantity supplied while depressing prices [1], [12]. The magnitude of price effects depends on the scale of disturbance relative to regional processing capacity, with large disturbances that exceed absorption capacity generating more severe price depressions [2], [6].

Second, salvage logging economics create a cost-price squeeze that severely compresses forest owner profitability. Salvage operations incur 10–64% higher costs than normal harvesting while simultaneously occurring during periods of depressed prices [3], [19]. This double burden can render salvage operations unprofitable, particularly for small forest owners with limited financial reserves [11]. The resulting loss of management motivation can lead to reduced forest stewardship and increased vulnerability to future disturbances [6], [11].

Third, the temporal dynamics of disturbance impacts follow characteristic patterns that vary by disturbance type. Windstorms create acute, high-volume supply shocks with immediate price effects that may persist for several years [1], [12]. Bark beetle outbreaks generate sustained, multi-year supply increases that prevent normal price recovery and create chronic market instability [5], [8]. Wildfires show more variable patterns, with some cases exhibiting price increases during salvage periods followed by longer-term supply effects [7], [24].

Fourth, cascading and interacting disturbances amplify overall impacts. Windstorms create breeding substrate for bark beetles, triggering secondary outbreaks that can exceed initial storm damage [30], [37]. Drought stress weakens tree defenses against bark beetles while increasing wildfire risk [8], [36]. These interactions extend the duration of market disruptions and complicate management responses [8], [19].

Regional vulnerabilities reflect differences in forest composition, climate exposure, management systems, and institutional capacity. Central Europe's Norway spruce-dominated forests have proven highly vulnerable to bark beetle outbreaks under drought and heat stress [5], [8], [11]. The concentration of spruce in even-aged plantations creates landscape-scale susceptibility when climatic conditions favor bark beetle population growth [8], [37]. Nordic and boreal regions have experienced lower impacts to date but face growing vulnerabilities as climate change extends bark beetle reproductive seasons and increases storm and wildfire risks [36], [38], [40].

Finland's specific vulnerabilities include: (1) increasing climate-driven disturbance risks that may exceed historical experience [36], [38]; (2) high salvage logging costs that compress profitability [3]; (3) value chain characteristics that have shown prolonged instability in response to disturbances [4]; and (4) tensions between intensive timber production and ecological sustainability that may be exacerbated by disturbances [34]. However, Finland also possesses adaptive capacity through advanced forest management expertise, well-developed harvesting technology, and integration into Nordic and European markets [42].

Adaptive capacity varies substantially across regions and stakeholder groups. Proactive management, diversified processing capacity, and coordinated governance enhance resilience [4]. Strategic timber storage can moderate price volatility by spreading salvage sales over time [12]. Market integration allows for timber export to less-affected regions, though it also means disturbance impacts can propagate across borders [9], [23]. Small forest owners face particular challenges due to limited financial reserves and reduced bargaining power during market disruptions [11].

The broader context of climate change fundamentally alters the disturbance regime and challenges traditional forest management paradigms. Historical disturbance patterns and management strategies may no longer be adequate as disturbance frequency and intensity increase [21], [36], [38]. Adaptation requires not only reactive responses to individual disturbances but also proactive measures to reduce vulnerability, enhance resilience, and maintain forest sector viability under changing conditions [4], [6], [37].

## 9. Future Directions and Policy Recommendations

The intensification of forest disturbances under climate change necessitates fundamental shifts in forest management, market governance, and policy frameworks. Several priority areas emerge from the evidence reviewed:

**Enhanced monitoring and early warning systems** are essential for timely disturbance detection and rapid response. Early detection of bark beetle infestations allows for targeted sanitation harvesting before populations explode [37]. Weather forecasting and climate projections can inform risk assessments and preparedness planning for windstorms and wildfires [36], [38]. Investment in remote sensing technologies and ground-based monitoring networks should be prioritized [35].

**Diversification of forest composition** can reduce landscape-scale vulnerability to specific disturbance agents. The concentration of Norway spruce in Central European forests has created systemic vulnerability to bark beetle outbreaks [8], [37]. Promoting mixed-species forests with greater diversity of age classes and structural complexity can enhance resilience while maintaining timber production [37]. However, diversification must be balanced against economic considerations and site suitability [37].

**Processing capacity and flexibility** are critical for absorbing sudden supply increases without catastrophic price collapses. Regions with diversified processing industries that can handle multiple timber assortments and qualities demonstrate greater market stability [4]. Investment in processing infrastructure, particularly in regions with high disturbance risk, can enhance adaptive capacity [4]. Flexibility to shift between products and markets provides additional resilience [4].

**Strategic timber storage infrastructure** can moderate price volatility by allowing salvage timber sales to be spread over time rather than flooding markets immediately [12]. Water storage facilities, dry storage yards, and other infrastructure require investment but provide valuable buffering capacity [12]. Storage strategies must be integrated with market management to optimize timing of timber sales [12].

**Coordinated market governance and policy interventions** can prevent market collapse and maintain forest owner viability during major disturbances. The Czech Republic and Austria required hundreds of millions of euros in state interventions to stabilize markets during bark beetle crises [5], [8]. Proactive policy frameworks that anticipate disturbances and provide coordinated responses demonstrate greater effectiveness than reactive, ad hoc measures [4]. International coordination may be necessary for large-scale disturbances affecting multiple countries [19].

**Economic support mechanisms** for forest owners can maintain management motivation and prevent abandonment of forest stewardship. Small forest owners are particularly vulnerable to the profitability squeeze created by high salvage costs and depressed prices [11]. Subsidies for salvage operations, price support mechanisms, and financial assistance programs can help maintain active management [8], [11].

**Climate change adaptation and mitigation strategies** must be integrated into forest policy. Reducing greenhouse gas emissions to limit climate change remains the fundamental long-term solution to reducing disturbance intensification [36], [38]. Adaptation measures include adjusting species selection, modifying rotation lengths, and implementing risk-reduction silviculture [37]. The tension between maximizing timber production and maintaining ecological sustainability requires careful navigation [34].

**Research priorities** include: (1) improved understanding of disturbance interactions and cascading effects; (2) development of market models that incorporate disturbance dynamics; (3) evaluation of adaptation strategies under different climate scenarios; (4) assessment of value chain resilience across diverse contexts; and (5) analysis of policy effectiveness in maintaining forest sector viability [18], [43]. Long-term monitoring of disturbance impacts and management responses is essential for adaptive learning [21].

**Finland-specific recommendations** include: (1) continued investment in salvage logging technology and methods to reduce costs [3]; (2) enhancement of value chain coordination to improve disturbance response [4]; (3) proactive planning for increasing disturbance risks under climate change [36], [38]; (4) integration of disturbance considerations into sustainable harvest level calculations [34]; and (5) strengthening of Nordic and European market linkages to enhance resilience [42].

## 10. Conclusion

Physical forest disturbances—bark beetle outbreaks, windstorms, and wildfires—exert profound and multifaceted impacts on wood availability and timber prices across European forests, with significant implications for boreal regions and Finland. The evidence synthesized in this review demonstrates that disturbances create involuntary supply shocks that depress timber prices by 20–70%, disrupt long-term forest management, and challenge the economic viability of forestry operations. Central Europe has experienced the most severe impacts, with bark beetle outbreaks generating nearly 100 million m³ of unplanned harvests and driving spruce prices to historical lows. Major windstorms have caused immediate market saturation and price collapses of 35–69% in affected regions. While Finland has experienced comparatively limited direct impacts to date, salvage logging costs are 10–64% higher than normal operations, and growing climate-driven risks threaten future stability.

The mechanisms underlying these impacts are well-established: disturbances shift timber supply curves rightward, salvage operations incur substantially higher costs while occurring during periods of depressed prices, and cascading disturbances extend the duration of market disruptions. Regional vulnerabilities reflect differences in forest composition, climate exposure, and institutional capacity, with Central Europe's spruce-dominated forests proving highly susceptible to bark beetle outbreaks under drought and heat stress.

Value chain resilience varies substantially across regions, with proactive management, diversified processing capacity, and coordinated governance enhancing stability. However, the intensification of disturbances under climate change challenges traditional management paradigms and necessitates fundamental adaptation. Priority responses include enhanced monitoring and early warning systems, diversification of forest composition, investment in processing capacity and storage infrastructure, coordinated market governance, economic support for forest owners, and integration of climate adaptation into forest policy.

For Finland specifically, the relatively limited historical disturbance impacts should not engender complacency. Climate projections indicate increasing risks from storms, bark beetles, and wildfires, while existing evidence demonstrates high salvage costs and value chain vulnerabilities. Proactive adaptation, including investment in salvage technology, enhancement of value chain coordination, and integration of disturbance considerations into sustainable harvest planning, will be essential for maintaining forest sector resilience in an era of accelerating environmental change.

The forest disturbances documented in this review represent not merely temporary market disruptions but fundamental challenges to the sustainability and economic viability of European forestry. Addressing these challenges requires coordinated action across multiple scales—from individual forest owners implementing risk-reduction silviculture to international policy frameworks supporting market stability and climate change mitigation. The capacity of European forest sectors to adapt to intensifying disturbances will determine not only the future of timber production but also the broader provision of forest ecosystem services in a changing climate.
