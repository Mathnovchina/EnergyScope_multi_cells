# Physical Disturbance Risks in Boreal Forests: Economic and Energy System Impacts in Finland

## Executive Summary

Boreal forests in Finland face a mounting portfolio of physical disturbances — windstorms, heavy snow loading, drought-driven wildfires, bark beetle outbreaks, and root-rot fungi — that are amplifying under climate change and increasingly interact with one another in damaging cascades. Because the Finnish forest sector generates roughly EUR 29.4 billion in annual output, accounts for about 17% of national export revenues, and provides the feedstock for over 70% of Finland's renewable energy, disturbance-induced shocks propagate well beyond the forest and into the broader economy and energy system. This report analyses each disturbance type, its economic transmission mechanisms, and its specific implications for Finland's biomass-dependent energy infrastructure.[^1][^2]

***

## 1. Principal Physical Disturbance Risks

### 1.1 Windstorms

Wind is the dominant abiotic damage agent in Finnish boreal forests. The most severe historical events — such as storms Tapani (2011) and Pyry (2011), each felling approximately four million cubic metres — and more recently Storm Hannes (December 2025, ~170,000 electricity customers disconnected) — illustrate both the frequency and infrastructure reach of this hazard. Wind damage risk is projected to increase especially in southern Finland, not because of intensifying wind speeds per se, but because of the shortening of the soil frost period: frozen ground anchors tree roots, and as winters warm, the frost window narrows, leaving trees more susceptible to uprooting. A key non-linearit is that wind-damaged areas produce large quantities of stressed, weakened, or dead trees that become ideal beetle breeding sites — directly linking abiotic and biotic disturbances in a cascade.[^3][^4][^5][^6][^7][^8]

### 1.2 Heavy Snow Loading and Ice Damage

Crown snow loading causes stem breakage, crown bending, and stand-level damage that reduces timber quality and merchantable volume. The geographical gradient of this risk is reversing under climate change: snow damage is anticipated to increase in northern Finland (where warming turns dry snow to wetter, heavier snow) and decrease in the south where milder winters bring rain rather than snow. Ice storms pose related but distinct threats, creating encasement events that damage young regeneration and suppress forest productivity. Finnish forestry infrastructure — particularly winter logging roads which depend on frozen ground — is also directly impaired by reduced or irregular snow cover and ground frost conditions.[^9][^10][^11][^3]

### 1.3 Drought and Wildfire

Fire has historically been rare in Finnish boreal forests relative to North American or Siberian boreal zones, but this is changing. Summer droughts are increasing in both frequency and intensity, extending fire-weather windows and drying fuels. Under unabated climate change, the area of European forest disturbed by all agents is projected to more than double by end of century, with wildfires being the disturbance most sensitive to warming, expanding from Mediterranean zones into temperate and boreal regions. A 2025 Wageningen University study confirms that changing tree densities are already increasing wildfire risk in boreal systems globally. For Finland, probabilistic studies show that the risk of large-scale fires is projected to increase substantially through drought-enhanced fuel drying.[^12][^5][^13][^14][^15]

### 1.4 Bark Beetle Outbreaks

The European spruce bark beetle (*Ips typographus*) is a particularly dangerous agent in Finnish Norway spruce stands. Unlike most other disturbances, it can attack and kill healthy trees once population densities cross a critical threshold, initiating self-sustaining outbreaks that have already killed hundreds of millions of trees in Central Europe. In boreal Finland, the beetle remains present but episodic; however, warming temperatures accelerate its developmental rate (enabling two generations per year instead of one in warm summers) and summer droughts weaken tree defenses, making outbreaks more likely. A 2026 study by the University of Eastern Finland mapped stand-level damage probability across 11.4 million hectares and found that large tree diameter, proximity to recent clear-cuts or prior infestations, and short heat waves all significantly elevated risk. A critical time-lag of approximately three years separates drought or stand-edge exposure events from peak beetle population numbers, complicating early intervention.[^16][^17]

### 1.5 Root Rot (*Heterobasidion* spp.)

Root and butt rot caused by *Heterobasidion* fungi causes chronic, long-term timber loss — reducing wood volume, inducing timber quality degradation, and, crucially, pre-weakening trees for wind throw. Warmer winters remove the cold-temperature check on fungal growth, widening its geographic range northward and lengthening its active season. Root rot therefore acts as a "background stressor" that amplifies the severity of both wind and beetle events.[^5][^3]

### 1.6 Cascading and Compound Events

Perhaps the most consequential finding from contemporary research is the strongly increasing probability of multi-agent cascades: a major windstorm produces large volumes of windthrown material that (i) weakens surviving stand-edge trees, (ii) provides ideal breeding substrate for bark beetles, and (iii) makes salvage logging necessary but operationally difficult. These cascades can decouple timber markets from planned supply expectations and impose compound losses across multiple years rather than a single event year.[^4][^5]

## 2. Impacts on the Forest Economy

### 2.1 Sectoral Importance

The forest sector is a central pillar of the Finnish economy. In 2024, forest industry exports were worth approximately EUR 12 billion, representing about 17% of Finland's total export revenue. The direct and indirect value added by the forest industry amounted to nearly EUR 11 billion, and the sector directly employs roughly 40,000–57,900 people, rising to approximately 83,000–100,000 with indirect employment. The forest sector's output — EUR 29.4 billion in 2024 — constitutes 36% of the entire Finnish bioeconomy. Disturbances that impair timber productivity therefore have macroeconomic consequences that extend far beyond the forest gate.[^18][^2][^1]

### 2.2 Timber Supply Disruption and Price Volatility

Physical disturbances disrupt planned harvesting schedules by generating large volumes of **salvage timber** — windthrown or beetle-killed wood that must be extracted urgently to prevent further value loss and beetle spread. Research on the Kainuu (Finland) forest value chain from 2001 to 2021 shows that a major windstorm and snow damage event in 2018 caused a notable spike in salvage logging, followed by delayed revenue responses in subsequent years as surplus timber was gradually introduced to markets. This mechanism produces timber market flooding, depressing sawlog and pulpwood prices temporarily while undermining long-term planning. European-wide evidence confirms that large-scale disturbances reduce value-added in lower-quality segments as high-value sawlogs are downgraded to fuelwood, while international trade partially compensates but cannot fully buffer domestic market effects.[^19][^20]

Price volatility is particularly acute for sawlogs and pulpwood, as shown by the Kainuu study — Finland experienced pronounced peaks around 2007–2010 followed by significant declines, partly shaped by market and disturbance interactions. Energy wood prices showed more stability but experienced a sharp drop after 2007 and only recovered by 2020–2021, illustrating how biomass energy supply chains are not fully insulated from disturbance-induced market disruptions.[^19]

### 2.3 Losses to Forest Owners and Downstream Industries

For Finland's more than 440,000 private forest owners, who hold about 62% of forest land and depend on stumpage earnings (EUR 3.0 billion in 2024), disturbance events can rapidly erode asset values. The economic cost of disturbance events at the European level currently amounts to EUR 115 billion in forestry industry losses from wildfires, storms, and bark beetles, projected to rise to EUR 247 billion under unabated climate change by end of century. Finland, as one of Europe's largest pulp and paper producers (forest industry exports of approximately EUR 15 billion in 2022), is deeply exposed to these dynamics.[^21][^22][^23][^1]

Downstream industries — pulp and paper mills, sawmills, wood product companies — depend on a steady volume and quality of timber supply. Finland's total industrial wood use is around 67 million m³ annually. Disturbances that divert supply quality (e.g., beetle-killed or wind-thrown spruce used for pulp rather than sawlogs) reduce the revenue per cubic metre and can trigger capacity shutdowns or underutilization in mills designed for higher-grade wood.[^24]

### 2.4 Increased Operational and Harvesting Costs

Climate-induced shortening of the soil frost period poses a distinct economic challenge: it reduces the number of days available for heavy harvesting machinery to operate on frozen ground, which in Finland is the standard logistical approach for timber extraction in wet peatland-rich terrain. More days of unfrozen ground translate directly into higher harvesting costs per cubic metre, requiring investment in alternative machinery or infrastructure. This cost increase disproportionately affects more remote and northern stands where alternative logistics are most limited.[^11]

### 2.5 Carbon Sink Degradation and Regulatory Risk

Boreal forests in the northern hemisphere, including Finland, shifted from net carbon sinks to net sources of carbon loss during 2016–2022, losing an average of 0.20 petagrams of carbon per year from living biomass. This trend — driven by droughts, wildfires, insect outbreaks, and permafrost thaw — has significant implications for Finnish climate policy, which assigns a major role to forest carbon sinks in meeting EU climate targets. Disturbance-induced carbon sink weakening exposes Finland to regulatory risk under the EU Land Use, Land Use Change and Forestry (LULUCF) accounting framework, potentially requiring compensatory emission reductions in other sectors.[^25]

## 3. Impacts on Energy Systems

### 3.1 Structural Dependence of Finnish Energy on Forest Biomass

Finland's energy system is uniquely exposed to forest disturbances because of its deep structural reliance on wood-based fuels. Over 70% of Finland's renewable energy is generated with wood-based fuels, and approximately two-thirds of renewable energy in Finland is linked to operations in the forest industries (primarily pulping sidestreams). In 2022, renewables represented 42% of total energy consumption in Finland, of which 70% was wood-based. District heating — which serves over 90% of apartment blocks and is the backbone of heating supply in Finnish urban centres — relies heavily on bioenergy, with roughly 49% of district heat produced from renewables. Any disruption to forest biomass supply, whether in volume or in feedstock quality, directly threatens energy security.[^26][^27][^24]

### 3.2 Direct Infrastructure Impacts of Windstorms and Snow Loads

Windstorms are the primary cause of power outages in Finland's electricity distribution network, with snow loads being the secondary abiotic factor. The mechanism is predominantly indirect: storms uproot or break trees, which then fall onto overhead distribution lines — a vulnerability particularly acute in rural and peri-urban Finland where the medium- and low-voltage network runs through forested landscapes. A strong positive correlation exists between wind speed and the number of power supply interruptions, and unfrozen (warming) soils increase this susceptibility by reducing tree anchorage. Storm Vieno (2021) left over 60,000 households without electricity across 100 municipalities; Storm Hannes (2025) disconnected approximately 170,000 customers.[^8][^28][^29][^30]

Reduced soil frost is therefore a double energy risk: it both increases tree windthrow (driving outages) and raises forest harvesting costs (constraining biomass availability). Climate projections point toward a future with more frequent and severe storms, heavy precipitation, and erratic snow loads, amplifying these dynamics.[^31]

### 3.3 Biomass Feedstock Disruption

When forest disturbances are large enough, they can disrupt the supply chains that feed district heating plants and bioenergy installations. The practical experience of Finland after Russia's 2022 invasion of Ukraine — which abruptly ended imports of Russian energy wood chips — demonstrated how supply chain disruptions can quickly threaten energy security: "Security of supply will be at stake unless we can get the timber out of the forest," warned bioenergy expert Kyösti Turkia of the Finnish Forest Centre. Physical disturbances impose analogous supply shocks through domestic channels. A major windstorm or bark beetle outbreak affecting large areas simultaneously can overwhelm salvage logging capacity, delay biomass delivery, and leave heating plants short of fuel during peak winter demand.[^26]

Over 60% of solid wood fuel used for energy comes from industrial sidestreams (bark, black liquor, sawdust); the remaining 40% must be harvested directly from forests, which is both the most expensive and the most logistically vulnerable component. Disturbances that damage stand structure or contaminate biomass (e.g., beetle-killed wood may lower energy quality) affect this direct harvesting portion particularly severely.[^26]

### 3.4 Carbon Feedback and the Biomass–Energy Tension

Large-scale forest disturbances create a fundamental tension for Finland's climate and energy policy. On the one hand, disturbance-killed trees represent an available biomass source that can be utilized for energy, partly displacing fossil fuels. On the other hand, forests recovering from disturbance absorb less carbon, and when disturbance-killed biomass is burned for energy, the carbon stored in those trees is released immediately — eliminating the carbon benefit typically attributed to biomass energy over time. Life-cycle carbon analyses for Finnish wood utilization suggest that wood energy decreases net carbon emissions with a probability lower than 40%, primarily because the reduction in forest carbon sink offsets fossil fuel substitution benefits. As disturbances intensify and carbon sink performance deteriorates, this trade-off becomes more severe.[^32][^33][^25]

### 3.5 Grid Resilience and Adaptation

Recognizing the growing threat, Finnish grid operators are undertaking significant infrastructure investments. Elenia, a major Finnish electricity distributor, has committed to undergrounding 75% of its power lines by 2028, having adopted a policy of building only weather-proofed underground distribution lines since 2009. Underground cables are largely immune to falling-tree damage and ice/snow accumulation that down overhead wires, offering a practical engineering response to the climate-driven upswing in storm events.[^31]

District heating systems are also diversifying away from pure biomass dependency. The emerging hybrid model integrates electric boilers, heat pumps, thermal storage, and waste heat recovery alongside bioenergy, providing greater flexibility to manage biomass supply shocks. This transition is motivated precisely by energy security concerns, including the need to maintain heat supply on the coldest days even when biomass availability is constrained.[^34][^35]

## 4. Compound Risks and Systemic Vulnerabilities

| Disturbance | Primary Forest Impact | Economic Channel | Energy System Impact |
|---|---|---|---|
| Windstorms | Windthrow, stand-edge exposure | Salvage logging surge, price volatility | Power outages, biomass supply surge then depletion |
| Snow/Ice loading | Crown breakage, quality loss | Reduced sawlog grade, harvesting difficulty | Grid outages, heating infrastructure damage |
| Drought/Wildfire | Stand mortality, soil carbon loss | Area loss, replanting cost, carbon accounting penalties | Biomass combustion releases stored carbon; wildfire risk to grid infrastructure |
| Bark beetle | Spruce mortality | Timber quality downgrade, pulp/fuelwood flooding | Short-term biomass surplus (beetle-killed wood), long-term supply reduction |
| Root rot | Chronic volume loss, wind predisposition | Long-term yield reduction | Reduced sustainable biomass harvest |
| Cascades (wind + beetle) | Compound multi-year mortality | Multi-year revenue and supply disruption | Prolonged feedstock uncertainty for bioenergy |

The probability of these compound disturbance events is projected to increase remarkably in Finnish boreal conditions under climate change. Forest disturbances across Europe are expected to result in a 14% increase in young forests and a 3% decrease in old forests by end of century under unabated emissions, restructuring the age class distribution and the temporal pattern of biomass availability in ways that challenge long-term energy planning.[^12][^4][^5]

## 5. Adaptive Responses and Policy Implications

### Forest Management Adaptation

Research consensus points toward several management interventions to reduce disturbance risk:

- **Diversification toward mixed-species stands**: Reducing pure Norway spruce monocultures limits both bark beetle susceptibility and wind damage at landscape scale.[^17][^16]
- **Continuous cover forestry**: Avoids creation of sharp stand edges that expose trees to wind and facilitate beetle dispersal, offering higher economic resilience than clear-fell systems.[^19]
- **Spatially targeted monitoring**: Stand-level risk maps (as produced for bark beetle probability across 11.4 million hectares in Finland in 2026) enable proactive salvage and prophylactic thinning before infestations establish.[^17]
- **Reduced-impact harvesting timing**: Earlier or more flexible harvesting scheduling to reduce the window of fresh beetle breeding substrate after windstorms.

### Energy System Resilience

- **Underground cabling**: Elenia's undergrounding programme targets 75% of lines by 2028, directly reducing storm-induced outages.[^31]
- **Hybrid heat production**: Integrating electric boilers, heat pumps, and thermal storage diversifies district heating away from biomass-only supply chains.[^35][^34]
- **Biomass supply chain diversification**: The post-Ukraine experience illustrates the value of maintaining multiple feedstock pathways (industrial sidestreams, young forest thinnings, agricultural residues) to buffer against any single supply disruption.[^26]
- **Strategic biomass reserves**: Bioterminals and on-site fuel storage at heating plants provide buffer stock for periods of harvest disruption.[^26]

### Broader Macroeconomic Exposure

Because forest disturbances can simultaneously impair timber supply, degrade carbon sinks, raise energy production costs, and interrupt electricity distribution, the Finnish economy faces a compound exposure that requires cross-sectoral policy coherence — linking forest management regulation, energy security policy, and climate accounting frameworks. The EU LULUCF regulation and evolving sustainability criteria for biomass add a regulatory layer: if Finnish forests become net carbon sources due to disturbance intensity, it may constrain the policy viability of wood-based energy precisely when biomass demand is highest.[^32][^25]

## **Duration of Bark Beetle Outbreaks in European Forests**

Bark beetle “invasions” (outbreaks) in European spruce forests usually last several years, often with a build‑up, a short peak, and then a decline. Their total duration and intensity depend strongly on climate (especially drought and heat), forest structure, and management.

### **Typical Outbreak Length and Phases**

- In the Czech Republic (national scale), major Ips typographus activity persisted for many years: low–moderate damage from 2003–2016, then a sharp intensification 2017–2019, for a total disturbance episode of about 17 years with a 3‑year extreme phase  (Hlásny et al. 2021, 119075).  
- Detailed satellite analysis in the Czech Republic (2016–2022) found “waves” of infestation with a **3‑year build‑up, 1 peak year, then decline** in most foci (about 5–6 years per wave)  (Washaya et al. 2024).  
- In Wallonia and NE France, the 2018 drought‑triggered outbreak **lasted 6 years (2017–2022)**  (Arthur et al. 2024).  
- In a national park in Germany, two distinct epidemics spanned 1996–2000 and 2005–2009; overall, ~44% of the landscape was disturbed across 23 years  (Seidl et al. 2016, 
          530-540
).

### **What Prolongs or Shortens Outbreaks?**

- **Drought and heat** strongly prolong and intensify outbreaks, allowing more generations and reducing tree defenses  (Hlásny et al. 2021, 119075; Das et al. 2025; Pirtskhalava-Karpova et al. 2024; Jaime et al. 2022, 2830 - 2841).  
- Uniform, spruce‑dominated, even‑aged forests support long‑lasting, self‑sustaining outbreaks  (Sommerfeld et al. 2020, 737 - 749; Hlásny and Turčáni 2013, 481 - 491).  
- Active sanitation and removal of wind‑felled trees within about **2 years after storms** can prevent transition to harder‑to‑control, patch‑driven outbreaks  (Økland et al. 2016, 63-73).  

## **Conclusion**

In European spruce forests, bark beetle outbreaks commonly last on the order of 5–10 years per wave, with some regions experiencing disturbance phases extending over one to two decades. Climate extremes and forest structure can make invasions longer and more severe, while rapid management after wind or early attacks can shorten or dampen them.

## **Bark beetles in European (boreal) spruce forests: what is happening?**

European boreal and temperate spruce forests are increasingly affected by outbreaks of the **European spruce bark beetle (Ips typographus)**. These outbreaks are tightly linked to climate (warming, drought), storm damage, and dense spruce-dominated stands.

## **Where and how big are the outbreaks?**

- Central Europe (Czech Republic, Austria, Germany, Poland) has seen unprecedented mortality of Norway spruce since about 2015, often affecting several percent of total growing stock per year and millions of cubic meters of timber  (Washaya et al. 2024; Hallas et al. 2024, 263 - 274; Stereńczak et al. 2020, 117826; Hlásny et al. 2021, 119075; Pirtskhalava-Karpova et al. 2024; Netherer et al. 2019).  
- A large, high‑latitude outbreak has also occurred in Siberian spruce forests in north‑west Russia, the northernmost recorded for this species  (Trubin et al. 2022).  
- In Sweden and other northern countries, major recent outbreaks followed extreme drought (e.g., 2018)  (Gohli et al. 2024; Kärvemo et al. 2025; Marini et al. 2017, 1426–1435; Hlásny et al. 2021, 119075).  
- Outbreak waves often show a 3‑year build‑up, 1 peak year, then decline, with large areas (thousands of km²) heavily disturbed and fragmented  (Washaya et al. 2024; Hallas et al. 2024, 263 - 274; Hlásny et al. 2021, 119075; Hlásny and Turčáni 2013, 481 - 491).

## **Ecology and dynamics**

- Beetles mass‑attack trees using pheromones; under high populations they can kill even relatively healthy trees  (Marini et al. 2017, 1426–1435; Das et al. 2025; Wermelinger 2004, 67-82; Hlásny et al. 2021, 138 - 165).  
- Outbreaks show **negative density dependence** (populations eventually crash) but can still transform landscapes by rapidly reducing spruce and forest cover  (Washaya et al. 2024; Marini et al. 2017, 1426–1435; Das et al. 2025; Hlásny et al. 2021, 119075; Hlásny and Turčáni 2013, 481 - 491).  
- Natural enemies (predatory beetles, parasitoids, fungi) exist, but their regulating effect is variable; some predators (e.g., Ple-gaderus spp.) can reduce beetle success locally  (Kärvemo et al. 2025; Wermelinger 2004, 67-82; Audsley et al. 2023).  

## **Management and future outlook in boreal Europe**

- In boreal Norway and Sweden, large outbreaks have so far been rarer than in Central Europe but risk is expected to rise with more extreme weather  (Gohli et al. 2024; Kärvemo et al. 2025; Marini et al. 2017, 1426–1435; Hlásny et al. 2021, 138 - 165).  
- Risk reduction options include lowering spruce proportion, reducing large clearcut edges, and focusing on early detection and sanitation felling of infested trees  (Gohli et al. 2024; Kautz et al. 2022, 403-414; Stereńczak et al. 2020, 117826; Hlásny et al. 2021, 138 - 165; Netherer et al. 2019; Fernández-Carrillo et al. 2024).  
- Modelling and remote sensing are increasingly used to predict hotspots and timing of outbreaks and to guide management  (Washaya et al. 2024; Trubin et al. 2022; Kautz et al. 2022, 403-414; Stereńczak et al. 2020, 117826; Lagergren et al. 2025; Pirtskhalava-Karpova et al. 2024; Hroššo et al. 2020; Fernández-Carrillo et al. 2024).

## **Summary**

Bark beetle outbreaks in European (including boreal) spruce forests are becoming more frequent and severe, driven mainly by warming, drought, and storm damage in dense, spruce‑rich stands. These outbreaks can rapidly kill vast areas of spruce, fragment forests, and trigger long‑term shifts in forest composition. Adaptive management that reduces stand vulnerability and improves early detection is central to limiting future impacts, especially as climate extremes intensify.

# **How Shocks Disturb the Forest Economy and Broader Economy: Insights and the Case of Energy Systems**

## 1. Introduction

Shocks—such as economic crises, climate extremes, pandemics, and energy disruptions—can significantly disturb both the forest economy and the broader economic system. These disturbances manifest through reduced production, price volatility, supply chain interruptions, changes in demand for forest products (including wood-based energy), and impacts on ecosystem services vital to society  (Schwarzbauer et al. 2013, 13-22; Wunder et al. 2021, 102536 - 102536; Mohr et al. 2025, 1078 - 1083; Imbrenda et al. 2023; Raihan 2023; Romagnoli et al. 2022). The effects are complex and context-dependent: while some shocks (like economic downturns) may reduce deforestation by lowering commodity prices, others (such as climate-induced disturbances or energy crises) can cause severe losses in timber value, disrupt livelihoods, and challenge the resilience of both forest-based sectors and energy systems  (Mohr et al. 2025, 1078 - 1083; Baker et al. 2022, 1 - 17; Cantarello et al. 2024, 1095 - 1108; García-Jácome et al. 2025). The interplay between forests and energy is particularly important; for example, increased demand for wood biomass as a renewable energy source can buffer some economic shocks but also introduces new vulnerabilities  (Schwarzbauer et al. 2013, 13-22; Jåstad et al. 2020, 113626; Majchrzak et al. 2022). Building resilience—through innovation, adaptive management, diversification, and integrated policy—is crucial for mitigating these impacts and ensuring sustainable development  (Hoeben et al. 2023, 301 - 318; García-Jácome et al. 2025; Nikinmaa et al. 2023,

## 3. Results

### 3.1 Types of Shocks Affecting Forest Economies

- **Economic Crises:** Economic downturns lead to declining production in forestry sectors (forestry, sawmills, paper), lower prices for timber products, reduced exports/imports balance, and shifts in domestic vs. export market reliance  (Schwarzbauer et al. 2013, 13-22; Wunder et al. 2021, 102536 - 102536).
- **Climate-Induced Disturbances:** Extreme events such as wildfires, storms, droughts, pest outbreaks are increasing in frequency/severity due to climate change. These cause direct losses in timber value (up to 42% reduction projected in Europe), disrupt supply chains, increase salvage logging needs, reduce carbon sequestration capacity (~24% decrease possible after major storms), and threaten sectoral stability  (Mohr et al. 2025, 1078 - 1083; Bastit et al. 2024; Mina et al. 2022, 4323 - 4341).
- **Pandemics/Health Shocks:** COVID-19 caused labor shortages due to illness/quarantine measures; mobility restrictions disrupted supply chains; demand shifted unpredictably; institutional changes affected forest management practices; negative economic outcomes included reduced park revenues/ecotourism income  (Wunder et al. 2021, 102536 - 102536; Kuuwill et al. 2025, 3565 - 3585).
- **Energy System Shocks:** Energy price spikes or supply disruptions propagate through the economy by raising costs for production/transportation/logistics; they also affect demand for wood biomass as an alternative fuel source  (Ciola et al. 2023; Jåstad et al. 2020, 113626).

### 3.2 Mechanisms of Disturbance Propagation

- **Supply Chain Disruptions:** Shocks can break established connections between producers/processors/markets—leading to cascading effects on employment/income throughout rural economies  (Piquer-Rodríguez et al. 2023, 1 - 15; García-Jácome et al. 2025).
- **Market Volatility:** Price fluctuations following disturbances (e.g., storms) initially depress timber prices due to salvage surpluses but later raise them due to inventory depletion; non-affected regions may benefit from higher prices while affected areas suffer losses  (Bastit et al. 2024).
- **Institutional Change:** Shocks often induce rapid changes in formal/informal institutions governing forests—sometimes eroding traditional practices or creating new regulations with mixed ecological/economic outcomes  (Kuuwill et al. 2025, 3565 - 3585; Johnstone and Schot 2023).

### 3.3 Resilience Factors & Adaptive Strategies

- **Innovation & Diversification:** Diversifying product portfolios (e.g., expanding into bioenergy or non-timber products), improving operational efficiency/flexibility along value chains enhances resilience against future shocks  (Hoeben et al. 2023, 301 - 318; García-Jácome et al. 2025).
- **Adaptive Management:** Proactive management (e.g., risk-sharing insurance mechanisms for storm damage) helps buffer short-term losses while supporting long-term sustainability  (Bastit et al. 2024).
- **Integrated Policy Approaches:** Coordinated policies linking forestry with energy/climate goals are essential—especially as forests play a dual role in providing raw materials for bioenergy while maintaining ecosystem services  (Cantarello et al. 2024, 1095 - 1108; Jåstad et al. 2020, 113626).

### 3.4 Energy Systems: Special Considerations

- **Energy-Forest Interactions:** Increased use of wood biomass for renewable energy can stabilize demand during fossil fuel crises but may also intensify pressure on forests if not managed sustainably  (Schwarzbauer et al. 2013, 13-22; Jåstad et al. 2020, 113626).
- **Resilience Assessment:** Modern frameworks emphasize not just risk assessment but also post-shock recovery/reconfiguration capacities—key metrics include resistance (minimizing loss), restabilization (rapid recovery), rebuilding/adaptation (long-term transformation)  (Gasser et al. 2019, 273 - 299; Ahmadi et al. 2021).
- **Distributional Effects:** Energy shocks have uneven impacts across sectors/regions/populations—requiring targeted policy responses to avoid exacerbating inequalities or undermining recovery efforts  (Ciola et al. 2023).

## 4. Discussion

The reviewed literature demonstrates that shocks—whether economic crises or climate-driven disturbances—have significant but variable impacts on both forest economies and broader economic systems. While some sectors show moderate resilience due to adaptive capacity or market flexibility (e.g., sawmills reallocating supply between export/domestic markets), others face severe risks from compounded disturbances or lack of institutional support  (Schwarzbauer et al. 2013, 13-22; Mohr et al. 2025, 1078 - 1083). The increasing frequency/intensity of climate-related events is especially concerning: projected disturbance-induced losses could halve timber value in Europe under severe scenarios if adaptation is lacking  (Mohr et al. 2025, 1078 - 1083). 

Energy system resilience is closely intertwined with forest sector stability: disruptions in one propagate through the other via resource flows/pricing/labor markets/policy feedbacks  (Jåstad et al. 2020, 113626; Ciola et al. 2023). Integrated approaches that combine risk assessment with proactive adaptation strategies—including diversification along value chains and cross-sectoral policy coordination—are essential for building long-term resilience.

However, knowledge gaps remain regarding the quantification of indirect/cascading effects across scales; many studies focus on immediate sectoral impacts without fully capturing broader social/institutional consequences or feedback loops between forests/energy/economy/society  (Romagnoli et al. 2022). There is also a need for more empirical research on how different types of shocks interact over time.

## 5. Conclusion

Shocks—including economic crises, climate extremes/disasters/pandemics/energy disruptions—disturb both forest economies and broader economic systems through direct losses (production/value/jobs), indirect/cascading effects (supply chain breakdowns/institutional change), market volatility (price swings/resource scarcity), altered demand patterns (bioenergy expansion), and challenges to ecosystem service provision. Building resilience requires integrated approaches spanning innovation/diversification/adaptive management/policy coordination across forestry-energy-economy interfaces.

# **Key Physical Disturbance Risks in Boreal Forests (with a Focus on Finland)**

## 1. Introduction

Boreal forests, such as those in Finland, face a range of important physical disturbance risks that are being intensified by climate change and human activities. The most significant abiotic (physical) disturbance agents include **windstorms**, **heavy snow loading**, **drought**, and **forest fires**. These risks interact with forest structure, management practices, and biotic threats (like insect outbreaks), often leading to cascading effects that can amplify overall forest vulnerability. In Finland, wind damage risk is projected to increase especially in the south due to shorter soil frost periods, while snow damage is expected to rise in the north. Droughts are likely to boost large-scale fire risk, and warmer conditions may also increase bark beetle outbreaks following physical disturbances. Human-driven disturbances such as clear-cutting further compound these risks by fragmenting habitats and reducing ecosystem resilience  (Venäläinen et al. 2020, 4178 - 4196; Romeiro et al. 2022; Ikonen et al. 2020, 685-707; Määttänen et al. 2022; Lacand et al. 2025, 68 - 79; Seidl et al. 2017,

## 3. Results

### 3.1 Major Physical Disturbance Agents

- **Windstorms:** Wind is the most significant abiotic disturbance agent in Finnish boreal forests by volume of timber damaged  (Valta et al. 2019). Wind damage risk is increasing due to shorter soil frost periods under climate change  (Venäläinen et al. 2020, 4178 - 4196; Romeiro et al. 2022; Ikonen et al. 2020, 685-707). Severe storms can cause millions of cubic meters of timber loss per event  (Valta et al. 2019).
- **Snow Loading:** Heavy snow loads cause extensive tree breakage and are particularly damaging in northern/eastern Finland; risk is projected to rise with wetter winters  (Venäläinen et al. 2020, 4178 - 4196; Romeiro et al. 2022; Räty et al. 2024; Suvanto et al. 2020).
- **Drought:** Increasing summer droughts elevate the risk of large-scale forest fires and reduce tree vigor  (Venäläinen et al. 2020, 4178 - 4196; Romeiro et al. 2022; Seidl et al. 2017, 
          395-402
).
- **Forest Fires:** Fire frequency and severity are expected to increase with climate warming; although currently rare due to suppression efforts, future projections indicate a doubling or tripling of large fires this century  (Venäläinen et al. 2020, 4178 - 4196; Seidl et al. 2017, 
          395-402
; Seidl et al. 2020; Lehtonen et al. 2015, 239-253; Kilpeläinen et al. 2010, 383-398).

### 3.2 Interactions & Cascading Effects

- Physical disturbances often trigger or amplify biotic threats—e.g., windthrow or snow breakage creates breeding substrate for bark beetles  (Venäläinen et al. 2020, 4178 - 4196; Romeiro et al. 2022).
- Cascading events (e.g., windstorm followed by bark beetle outbreak) are increasingly likely under climate change scenarios  (Venäläinen et al. 2020, 4178 - 4196).

### 3.3 Spatial Patterns & Regional Variation

- Southern Finland: Higher wind damage risk due to more vulnerable Norway spruce stands and reduced soil frost  (Ikonen et al. 2020, 685-707).
- Northern/Eastern Finland: Increased snow damage risk due to heavier wet snow events  (Räty et al. 2024; Suvanto et al. 2020).
- Fire risk is highest during spring/early summer but may shift with changing precipitation patterns  (Lehtonen et al. 2015, 239-253).

### 3.4 Human-Induced Disturbances

- Clear-cutting and intensive forestry fragment habitats and reduce resilience against natural disturbances  (Määttänen et al. 2022).
- Timber harvesting on unfrozen soils increases soil compaction/rutting risks  (Toivio et al. 2017, 22-30).

## 4. Discussion

The reviewed literature consistently identifies windstorms, heavy snow loading (especially wet snow), droughts leading to increased fire risk, and direct fire events as the primary physical disturbance agents threatening boreal forests in Finland  (Venäläinen et al. 2020, 4178 - 4196; Romeiro et al. 2022; Seidl et al. 2017, 
          395-402
). These risks are intensifying due to climate change—particularly through warmer winters (reducing soil frost), wetter conditions (increasing heavy snow events), hotter/drier summers (boosting fire danger), and increased storm frequency/intensity.

Cascading effects—such as windthrow followed by bark beetle outbreaks—are a growing concern for forest health and management planning  (Venäläinen et al. 2020, 4178 - 4196). While active fire suppression has kept recent fire areas low compared to North America or Russia  (Määttänen et al. 2022), projections suggest that both frequency and severity will rise substantially this century if current trends continue  (Lehtonen et al. 2015, 239-253).

Human activities such as clear-cutting exacerbate these vulnerabilities by fragmenting landscapes and reducing ecological resilience; harvesting on unfrozen soils further increases soil compaction/disturbance risks  (Toivio et al. 2017, 22-30). Adaptive management strategies—including species diversification in regeneration and spatially targeted thinning—are recommended but require careful balancing of biodiversity conservation with production goals  (Repo et al. 2024, 
          123124
).

## 5. Conclusion

Physical disturbance risks—especially windstorms, heavy snow loading/wet snow events, drought-induced fires—and their interactions with biotic agents like bark beetles represent major challenges for Finnish boreal forests under climate change. Regional variation exists: southern areas face higher wind/fire risk; northern/eastern regions face more severe snow damage.

### Research Gaps

Despite extensive monitoring/data from Finland’s forests:
- There is limited long-term data on cumulative/cascading effects.
- Few studies integrate multiple disturbance types at landscape scale.
- More research needed on adaptive management effectiveness under future climate scenarios.
